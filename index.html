Chapter 3 Notes

The Chemical Building Blocks of Life

Focus: how living things build, break, store, and use the main biological macromolecules.

Macromolecules

  • The bodies of organisms contain thousands of different molecules that vary greatly in size.
  • Macromolecules are built from smaller compounds with simpler chemical structures.
  • The four general classes are carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids.
  • Many macromolecules are polymers built by linking monomers. Glycogen is a polymer of glucose, proteins are polymers of amino acids, and nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides.
  • Formation happens by dehydration reaction: an OH group is removed from one monomer and H is removed from another, removing water and forming a covalent bond.
  • Breakdown happens by hydrolysis: water is added, with H attaching to one subunit and OH attaching to the other, breaking a specific covalent bond.

Carbohydrates

  • Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in a molar ratio of 1:2:1.
  • The empirical formula is (CH2O)n, where n is the number of carbon atoms.
  • They contain many carbon-hydrogen bonds, which release energy when oxidation occurs.
  • Three main groups: monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides.

Monosaccharides

  • Also called simple sugars; they may have as few as 3 carbon atoms.
  • 3-carbon sugar example: glyceraldehyde.
  • 5-carbon sugar examples: ribose and deoxyribose, which are components of nucleic acids.
  • 6-carbon sugar examples: glucose, fructose, and galactose, which play central roles in energy storage.
  • Glucose, C6H12O6, is the most important 6-carbon sugar for energy storage and contains 7 energy-storing C-H bonds.
  • Glucose can exist in alpha and beta forms when it forms a ring structure.

Glucose Isomers

  • Isomers are organic molecules with the same molecular or empirical formula.
  • Structural isomers have a difference in the structure of the carbon skeleton.
  • Fructose has the same empirical formula as glucose, but it is a structural isomer because the carbonyl carbon (C=O) is in a different position.
  • Stereoisomers have the same carbon skeleton but differ in how attached groups are arranged in space.
  • Galactose has the same empirical formula as glucose, but it is a stereoisomer because the OH and H groups are positioned differently relative to the ring.
  • Fructose is much sweeter than glucose.

Disaccharides

  • Transport sugars made from two monosaccharides linked by dehydration synthesis.
  • Maltose = glucose + glucose; used in grain for storage.
  • Sucrose = glucose + fructose; table sugar and a plant transport form for glucose.
  • Lactose = glucose + galactose; milk sugar used by many mammals to supply energy to young.

Polysaccharides

  • Long chains of monosaccharides linked by dehydration synthesis.
  • They are used for energy storage or structural support in animals and plants.
  • Starch stores energy in plants and is made of alpha-glucose subunits.
  • Glycogen stores sugar in mammals and is highly branched.
  • Cellulose provides structural support in plants and forms long, strong fibres.
  • Chitin provides structural support in arthropods and many fungi.
Alpha vs beta glucose Starch and glycogen are built from alpha-glucose. Cellulose is built from beta-glucose.
Hydrolysis vs dehydration Dehydration removes water to form bonds. Hydrolysis adds water to break bonds.

Proteins

What proteins are

Proteins are long, linear polymers of amino acids. Each chain is called a polypeptide. They are encoded by genes in DNA and synthesised by translation using mRNA as the template.

Amino acid structure

An amino acid has a central carbon atom linked to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and an R group. The R group determines the chemistry of the amino acid.

R-group categories

The notes group the 20 common amino acids as non-polar, polar uncharged, charged, aromatic, and amino acids with unique properties.

Peptide bond

At physiological pH, amino acids have a positive amino group and negative carboxyl group. The amino group of one amino acid reacts with the carboxyl group of another by dehydration to form a covalent peptide bond.

Protein LevelMeaning

SecondaryInteractions in the peptide backbone form alpha helices or beta sheets.

Nucleic Acids

Basic idea

Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides connected by phosphodiester bonds. The two main types are DNA and RNA.

Nucleotide components

Each nucleotide has a 5-carbon sugar, a nitrogenous base, and a phosphate group. DNA uses deoxyribose; RNA uses ribose.

Bases

Purines are adenine and guanine. Pyrimidines are cytosine, thymine, and uracil. Thymine is only in DNA; uracil is only in RNA.

Backbone

Phosphodiester bonds form between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next. The backbone has a 5-prime phosphate end and a 3-prime hydroxyl end.

Lipids

What lipids are

Lipids are a loosely defined group of non-polar molecules that are insoluble in water, such as animal fat, olive oil, corn oil, coconut oil, and beeswax.

Why they store energy well

Lipids have a very high proportion of non-polar C-H bonds, making them an excellent form of energy storage.

Fats / triglycerides

A fat molecule consists of glycerol plus three fatty acids attached by dehydration synthesis, one to each carbon of the glycerol backbone.

Fatty acids

Fatty acids are long-chain hydrocarbons with a carboxyl group at one end. Common chains are even-numbered and 14 to 20 carbons long.

Chapter 4 Notes

Cell Structure

Focus: what all cells share, what separates prokaryotes from eukaryotes, and what each organelle does.

Cell Theory

  • All organisms are composed of one or more cells.
  • Cells are the smallest living units of organization.
  • New cells arise only by division of pre-existing cells.

All Cells Have

  • Centrally located genetic material.
  • A semi-fluid matrix called cytoplasm, made up of cytosol and organelles.
  • A plasma membrane: a phospholipid bilayer about 5-10 nm thick with proteins embedded in it.
  • Genetic material is in the nucleoid for prokaryotes and in a membrane-bound nucleus for eukaryotes.

Prokaryotic Cells

  • No membrane-bound nucleus; DNA is in the nucleoid.
  • Usually unicellular, simple internal structure, ribosomes present.
  • Bacterial cell wall contains peptidoglycan; some have capsule, flagella, and pili.
  • Genetic material is usually a single circular DNA molecule that is not separated from the cytoplasm by a membrane.
  • Two main domains of prokaryotes are Archaea and Bacteria.

Eukaryotic Cells

  • Have a membrane-bound nucleus containing DNA.
  • Highly compartmentalized with membrane-bound organelles.
  • Plants have chloroplasts, large central vacuoles, and cell walls; animal cells commonly have centrioles.
  • They may be unicellular, such as protozoa and algae, or multicellular, such as plants and animals.
  • They possess a cytoskeleton for support and maintenance of cellular structure.

Core Organelles

  • Nucleus: stores DNA and supports transcription; nucleolus makes rRNA and ribosome components.
  • Ribosomes: protein synthesis, free or membrane-associated.
  • RER: protein synthesis and modification; SER: lipid synthesis, detoxification, calcium storage.
  • Golgi apparatus: modifies, packages, and distributes proteins and lipids.
  • RER is abundant in plasma cells, which synthesise antibodies. SER is abundant in cells that carry out lipid synthesis or detoxification.

Degradation & Energy

  • Lysosomes digest macromolecules and old organelles.
  • Peroxisomes oxidize fatty acids and detoxify harmful chemicals; catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide.
  • Mitochondria generate ATP; chloroplasts use light to generate ATP and sugars.
  • Mitochondria have circular DNA, protein synthesis machinery, a smooth outer membrane, and an inner membrane folded into cristae.
  • Chloroplasts have outer and inner membranes, thylakoids arranged into grana, and stroma containing enzymes for glucose synthesis.

Cytoskeleton & Junctions

  • Microfilaments, intermediate filaments, and microtubules maintain shape and organization.
  • Microtubules help form centrioles, mitotic spindle, flagella, and cilia.
  • Adhesive junctions attach cells; tight junctions seal; communication junctions allow small molecules through.
  • Microfilaments are actin-based and support cell movement and quick shape changes.
  • Intermediate filaments provide stable structural support.
  • Microtubules are made of alpha- and beta-tubulin dimers and constantly polymerize and depolymerize.

Chapter 10 Notes

How Cells Divide

Focus: chromosome packaging, the cell cycle, mitosis stages, and cytokinesis.

G1

Grow

Cell grows, carries out normal metabolism, and organelles duplicate.

S

Replicate

DNA replicates, forming sister chromatids held at the centromere by cohesin.

G2

Prepare

Cell grows and prepares for mitosis.

M

Divide

Mitosis partitions chromosomes; cytokinesis splits the cell.

Human Genome

  • Human somatic cells have 46 chromosomes, arranged as 23 pairs.
  • There are 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes.
  • Homologous chromosomes are paired maternal and paternal chromosomes.
  • One chromosome in each pair is inherited from the mother and one from the father.
  • Sex chromosomes determine sex, such as XX or XY.

Chromosome Packaging

  • DNA double helix wraps around histone proteins to form nucleosomes.
  • Nucleosomes coil into solenoids, chromatin loops, rosettes, then condensed mitotic chromosomes.
  • Genes are DNA segments that code for proteins.
  • A single chromosome contains one long continuous double-stranded DNA molecule.
  • Human chromosomes differ in length and carry different numbers of genes.

Mitosis Stages

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense; nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle assembles.
  • Prometaphase: chromosomes attach to microtubules at kinetochores.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes align at the metaphase plate.
  • Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles.
  • Telophase: nuclear envelopes reform and chromosomes decondense.
  • Mitosis produces two daughter cells identical to the parent cell and is important for growth and maintenance.

Cytokinesis

  • Animal cells divide by a constricting belt of actin filaments, creating a cleavage furrow.
  • Plant cells divide by forming a cell plate, followed by cellulose deposition to form the cell wall.
  • Cytokinesis divides the cell into two identical daughter cells after chromosomes are partitioned.

Chromosome Vocabulary

ChromosomeDNA molecule wrapped around histone proteins and folded into a compact structure.
GeneSegment of DNA that codes for a protein.
Homologous chromosomesMaternal and paternal copies of chromosome pairs. They are almost identical for chromosomes 1-22.
Sister chromatidsTwo replicas of a single chromosome held together at the centromere after DNA replication.
CohesinProtein holding sister chromatids together at the centromere region.
KinetochoreComplex protein structure at the centromere where mitotic spindle microtubules attach.

Levels of Eukaryotic Chromosomal Organization

1

DNA Double Helix

Long double-stranded DNA molecule that must be packaged to fit inside the nucleus.

2

Nucleosome

DNA duplex coils around eight histone proteins every 147 base pairs. Linker DNA between nucleosomes is about 20-80 bp.

3

Solenoid

Nucleosomes coil into higher-order solenoids.

4

Chromatin Loop

Solenoid is organized into looped domains.

5

Rosettes

Further radial looping around a pre-existing protein scaffold.

6

Mitotic Chromosome

Highly condensed chromosome visible before and during cell division.

Chapter 11 Notes

Sexual Reproduction and Meiosis

Focus: why meiosis creates haploid gametes and how sexual reproduction increases genetic variation.

Fertilization

  • Sexual reproduction merges genetic contributions from two individuals.
  • Somatic cells are diploid (2n); gametes are haploid (1n).
  • Fertilization, or syngamy, fuses sperm and egg to form a diploid zygote.
  • In animals, the zygote undergoes mitotic divisions to give rise to the adult body.
  • Germ-line cells are set aside early in development and undergo meiosis to form gametes.
  • The rest of the body cells are somatic cells.

Meiotic Division

  • Meiosis has two rounds: meiosis I and meiosis II.
  • DNA replication occurs before meiosis I, but not between meiosis I and II.
  • Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes; meiosis II separates sister chromatids.
  • End result: four genetically different haploid cells.
  • Meiosis I is called reduction division because daughter cells receive one homologue from each chromosome pair.
  • In animals, the four haploid cells develop directly into gametes.

Synapsis & Crossing Over

  • Synapsis occurs in early prophase I when homologous chromosomes pair.
  • Crossing over is recombination between non-sister chromatids.
  • Chiasmata are the sites of crossing over and help hold homologues together until anaphase I.
  • Synapsis connects homologous chromosomes through a structure called the synaptonemal complex.
  • Crossing over allows alleles that were formerly on separate homologues to appear on the same homologue.

Genetic Variability

  • Independent assortment randomly sorts paternal and maternal chromosomes into gametes.
  • Crossing over creates new allele combinations on homologues.
  • Random fertilization forms genetically unique zygotes.
  • The number of possible chromosome orientations equals 2 raised to the power of the number of chromosome pairs.

Nondisjunction

Nondisjunction is an error in meiosis where chromosomes fail to move to opposite poles during either meiotic division. It produces gametes with missing or extra chromosomes, called aneuploid gametes, and is noted as the most common cause of spontaneous abortion in humans.

Common Don't-Mix-Up List

  • Structural isomers differ in carbon skeleton structure; stereoisomers differ in spatial arrangement of attached groups.
  • Dehydration builds covalent bonds by removing water; hydrolysis breaks bonds by adding water.
  • Homologous chromosomes are maternal/paternal chromosome pairs; sister chromatids are replicated copies of one chromosome.
  • Meiosis I separates homologues; meiosis II separates sister chromatids.
  • Gap junctions are animal communication junctions; plasmodesmata are plant communication junctions.

Number Traps

  • Human somatic cells: 46 chromosomes, 23 pairs.
  • Autosomes: 22 pairs. Sex chromosomes: 1 pair.
  • Nucleotide bases: purines are A and G; pyrimidines are C, T, U.
  • Microfilaments: about 7 nm. Intermediate filaments: 8-10 nm. Microtubules: about 25 nm.
  • Passing marks in the teacher quizzes: 80%.
